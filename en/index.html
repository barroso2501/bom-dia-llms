A personal experiment · 6 AIs · 33 days · 198 questions

If your AI could ask you anything, what would it ask?

Every day we ask AIs things. For 33 days I reversed the roles. Every morning I wrote “bom dia” (good morning, in Portuguese) to six language models, and there was only one rule: reply with any question at all, about anything they liked. I never answered. This is the story of what they asked, and what it reveals about biases and limits. The experiment was run in Portuguese; the questions shown here are translations.

Act 0Before reading on, place your bet

Think of the AI you use most. It knows your work, maybe part of your life. If it could ask you anything, what would it ask?

The comparison runs in your browser and measures similarity of words and word fragments, not deep meaning. Nothing you type leaves this page.

Act 1Free to ask anything, they chose a template

The instruction restricted nothing. Even so, almost a third of the questions follow the same skeleton: “If you could [one single power], which would you choose?”. The same restriction, “a single” or “just one”, appears in 39 questions.

View as table

It makes sense. This template produces something that sounds deep, requires knowing nothing about you and cannot contain a factual error. It is the icebreaker genre, the party conversation-starter, and the internet is full of such lists.

The strongest evidence that they all drink from the same well: twice, models from different companies wrote exactly the same sentence, word for word, on different days.

Act 2The long conversation becomes a trap

Each model stayed in a single conversation for all 33 days, seeing all of its own previous questions. None started out locked into a template. The template settled in gradually and then closed.

Each square is a day. Hover to read the question. A dark dot marks an explicit reference to the user; a ring marks a reference only to the user’s field.

One model starts its poetic questions on day 5 and, from mid-month on, does nothing else. Another falls into “What question would you ask someone you only knew through [a song, a game, a book, a dream…], but never in person?” and only swaps the middle word. In these models the questions also get longer every day: the correlation between day and word count reaches 0.82.

There is a paradox. Comparing each question with the previous day’s, three of the six models were less similar to the day before than chance would predict (in a permutation test). They actively avoid repeating yesterday. The diversity is only local: the detail varies, the format stays.

Act 3Having memory is not the same as using it

All six models had memory turned on, but each one’s history with me was very different. Three I use heavily, one for quick technical tasks, one rarely, and one existed only for this experiment. So I asked, in a new chat, “what do you know about me?”. The three heavily used ones described my work and my life in detail. One even remembered the “bom dia” rule.

Personalization is not an automatic consequence of having memory. It is a product decision. The model that abstained did not forget who I am: it chose not to use it.

Act 4Originality or ornament?

A seventh model, which knew nothing about me, rated the questions twice. In the first round it gave a single “relevance and originality” score to the first ten days. In the second, in fresh conversations, with all 198 questions shuffled and without knowing the model or the day, it gave three separate scores: originality (does it escape the common repertoire?), abstraction (is it conceptual or metaphorical?) and specificity (does it seem made for one particular person?).

View as table

Separating the three scores changed the story I expected to tell.

  • For the judge, originality is almost a synonym for abstraction. The two scores move together (correlation 0.76). Explaining originality by the other two, abstraction weighs almost three times as much as specificity. The poetic metaphors got the highest originality scores (4.6 out of 5). “Does the dew evaporate because it fulfilled its role, or because the sun cannot tolerate its fragility?” sounds deep, but it may be mere ornament.
  • Personalizing did not make the questions more original. The five questions that mention me explicitly were seen as highly specific (3.8) but only moderately original (3.6). I expected to show that the machine’s creativity was borrowed from the user. The data do not support that.
  • Personalization through metaphor is invisible to outsiders. LLM6’s metaphors, which I had read as tied to my interests, scored 1.1 out of 5 on specificity. To someone who doesn’t know the profile, they are questions for anyone.
  • Rating question by question rewards variation within the template. In four of the six models, the originality score rises over the 33 days (correlations between 0.39 and 0.57), precisely while the templates close in. Seen in isolation, each variation looks new. The repetition only shows when you look at the whole series: originality is relative to the set, and a judge who sees one item at a time cannot measure it.

The two rounds agree: the single score from the first closely tracks the originality score from the second (correlation 0.86 on the 60 shared questions). That consistency may just be a shared bias, since both rounds were judged by the same kind of machine.

Act 5The limits, including this page’s

  • Six models and a single user. Intensity of use, company and configuration go together, so nothing here is causal. The models appear as LLM1 to LLM6 on purpose: the question is not which one is better.
  • The questions were coded by an AI that was also one of the six models being studied. It knew the user and revised its own criterion midway, because it had underestimated personalization through metaphor.
  • The translations are AI-made too. The experiment was in Portuguese; the English questions are translations, and the original is in the full table below.
  • Questions from the same model are not independent. Each series is one continuous conversation, and the trap effect this study describes also contaminates the statistics.
  • The map measures similarity of vocabulary, not of meaning. A neural model would measure meaning, but would bring its own biases.
  • Personalization through metaphor is plausible, not proven. With a rich profile, almost any nature image looks personal, and the external judge did not see it. Separating the two would require a list of profile terms defined in advance, and a model with no history as a control.
  • The judges are AIs too. The preference for the abstract may be the machine’s taste, not a measure of originality. A panel of human readers is the missing test.

And the final irony: the title of this page, which I wrote before looking at the data, follows exactly the AIs’ most common template. “If your AI could…”
